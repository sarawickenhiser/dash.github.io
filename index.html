Abstract

Robotic minimally invasive surgery offers well-documented clinical benefits, but the cost and infrastructure requirements of purpose-built platforms limit access in rural and lower-resourced facilities. Recent work has teleoperated general-purpose robots for laparoscopic tasks and in vivo procedures, but relied on handheld instruments coupled through passive linkages rather than native robotic actuation.

Instead, we adapt da Vinci Classic and Xi instruments onto general-purpose robots that can integrate in clinical workflows. We present DASH, a da Vinci Adapter for Serial-link and Humanoid platforms, consisting of two types of adapters that require no modification to the instruments themselves. Each adapter is compatible with many robotic platform load capacities, engages the instrument's native latch, and wirelessly identifies inserted tools to load instrument-specific kinematics and coupling matrices. Teleoperated experiments demonstrate the efficacy of DASH and its benefits for enabling research access to surgical robotic platforms.

Overview

Purpose-built surgical robots need dedicated operating suites. DASH decouples the da Vinci instruments from the proprietary system so that compact, general-purpose robots can carry them. Both adapters use the instrument's own latching mechanism, identify the tool wirelessly, and adjust to its coupling matrix. Our contributions are:

  • The design and fabrication of separate da Vinci Classic and Xi tool adapters compatible with serial-link and humanoid platforms.
  • A wireless tool-identification pipeline that loads instrument-specific kinematics and calibration on insertion.
  • Quantitative evaluations on a humanoid platform against the previous humanoid laparoscopic system, showing large improvements in operational responsiveness and RCM control precision.

System Design

DASH C (Classic)

A guiding track and front support engage the Classic tool's built-in features to constrain lateral motion. Inserting the tool presses a spring-loaded latch plate down. Once the tool is seated, the springs push the plate back up and lock it in place. Pressing the tool's release disengages it.

DASH Xi

Snap-fit tabs grab the Xi tool's native latch. A 3 mm wave spring in each engagement cap gives up to 2 mm of compression, so misaligned disks yield instead of damaging the tool. Up to five motors drive the instrument; the springs are resin-printed in Gray V5.

Both motor packs use an OpenRB-150 controller, a PN532 NFC RFID reader, and DYNAMIXEL XC330-T181-T motors on a TTL bus at 11.1 V. Each adapter weighs under 0.5 kg with a tool inserted, which fits the payload of humanoids such as the Unitree G1 and arms such as the Franka Emika Panda.

Robot-platform interface

Each adapter bolts to an interchangeable wrist mount on its underside. One side of the mount fits DASH, and the other fits the robot's end effector, so the same electronics move between platforms. This work uses mounts for the Unitree G1 humanoid and the Franka Emika Panda.

Wireless tool identification and teleoperation

A two-state controller switches between searching for a tool and bridging commands to the motors. When the RFID reader detects a tool, the system loads its joint limits, shaft lengths, and coupling matrix. An auto-zero routine then rotates each motor ±50° and back so the spring caps interlock with every disk before teleoperation starts. Removing the tool returns the system to searching.

The surgeon console (MTM) publishes handle poses over ROS. Handle position drives the robot arm, and handle orientation drives the DASH motors. Arm inverse kinematics enforce a remote center of motion (RCM): the residual between the shaft line and the RCM point is driven to zero, and the four redundant joints of the 7-DOF arm are resolved with a posture objective. The shaft must stay within a 10 mm diameter around the RCM.

Command-execution tracking

Commanded to trace a 40 mm circle at the tool tip, the humanoid with DASH produced a 41.45 ± 0.30 mm radius over three trials. The previous system, with handheld Livsmed tools and a physically constrained RCM, produced 49.83 ± 1.09 mm.

RCM convergence

OptiTrack markers on either side of the RCM tracked the shaft over random trajectories. The two estimated RCMs sit 15.79 mm apart on a 410 mm shaft, with pivot errors of 3.37 ± 2.44 mm and 2.64 ± 1.54 mm, inside the 10 mm bound.

Xi coupling matrices

Xi coupling matrices are not public, so we measured them by tracking tool motion in 3D with Kinovea from several camera angles. Near-zero slopes in angular error show acceptable coupling accuracy for the Mega SutureCut Needle Driver and Tenaculum Forceps.

Simulation and initialization

A simulation built from ground-truth dimensions validated the full pipeline, from RCM initialization to MTM teleoperation. The robot moves from a neutral pose to place the tool at the predefined RCM and insertion depth, then hands control to the surgeon console.

Conclusion

Surgical-grade da Vinci Classic and Xi instruments can be reliably decoupled from the da Vinci platform and operated on general-purpose robots with DASH. For healthcare, this opens a more realistic path toward robotic assistance in hospitals without dedicated surgical robots. For robotics researchers, mounting clinical-grade instruments on widely available humanoid and serial-link robots lowers the barrier to surgical robotics research without a full da Vinci installation or the da Vinci Research Kit. DASH has not yet been validated on a full surgical task, under sterilization constraints, or with a surgeon at the console.
